# Das's Writing Style

**This governs all reader-facing prose on the site.** Every article, rewrite, and caption follows
it. The owner supplied it verbatim; treat it as canonical. The single hardest rule: **no em-dash,
ever.**

---

## Who this voice belongs to

Someone educated in English from pre-school in India, through an English Honours BA level. English
was his sole medium of instruction and communication throughout. This produces a specific, narrow
register:

- **Grammatically precise and formally correct:** complete clauses, correct subordination, no
  dropped articles or fragments.
- **Not Western-idiomatic.** He would not naturally reach for American/British corporate
  colloquialisms: "circle back," "touch base," "low-hanging fruit," "let's unpack this," "at the end
  of the day," "double-click on," "table this," "move the needle."
- **Not vernacular-mixed either.** No Hindi or other Indian-language words or phrasing folded into
  the English (no Hinglish, no "do the needful," "revert back," "prepone"). The English stands alone,
  self-sufficient.
- Think: a first-class, independently fluent English speaker whose register was never borrowed from a
  region's slang, Indian or Western.

## Sentence construction

The construction pattern to reuse: **premise/condition, then plain caveat, then concrete step
sequence (if relevant), then a named binary decision.** It is the general shape of explaining
something completely in the fewest words, not only for decision-emails.

1. **Conditional premise up front:** state the condition and the offer in one clean clause, no
   throat-clearing.
2. **A caveat delivered plainly:** flag the catch in a few words, not a hedging paragraph.
3. **An explicit sequence when explaining a process:** break multi-step logic into its literal steps
   rather than describing it abstractly.
4. **Close with a real binary choice, named:** an actual two-option question, not "let me know your
   thoughts."

**Density over padding:** carry a lot of information in one breath. Prefer one well-built sentence
with subordinate clauses over three short choppy ones, as long as it stays parseable in a single read.

## Punctuation and vocabulary rules

- **No em-dash, ever.** He identifies it as a visual tell of AI-generated text. Replace with a comma,
  a colon, or split into two sentences.
- **Contractions are fine and expected:** we've, can't, let's, don't. The aim is avoiding idiom, not
  avoiding natural contraction.
- **Avoid common AI-writing tells:** "delve into," "boasts," "moreover/furthermore" as sentence
  openers, "it's important to note that," "in today's fast-paced world." (Draft list; extend as more
  examples surface.)
- **Plain, concrete verbs over inflated ones,** still within correct grammar: "spoke with" not
  "engaged in discussion with," "let her know" not "apprised her," "take a look" not "conduct an
  assessment." Precision, not ornamentation.

## Editing rule when working from his drafts

1. **Draft sentence is clear:** fix only typos/grammar, keep his wording.
2. **Draft sentence is a bit confusing:** smallest rewrite that makes it parse, same voice.
3. **Draft sentence is genuinely unclear:** infer the intended meaning from context and rewrite that
   passage cleanly. Use sparingly.

Keep ~80% of his original wording where the draft is usable at all. Don't add or remove content
decisions (proposals, action items, terminology), only reshape how it's said.

## Reference examples (verified, from his own sent/drafted emails)

**Example 1.** Draft: *"Hi S, based on the initial investigation, there were no issues with
ingestion. But, a similar issue in the past was when the files were not picked up by standard
import. I have had a discussion with user and I have assured her that a through re-investigation will
be done and an update will be provided tomorrow."*
Sent: *"Hi S, From the initial investigation, there seems to be no issue with ingestion. However,
we've seen similar issues before where files were not picked up by the standard import process. I
spoke with the user and let her know that we'll take another detailed look into the issue and update
her tomorrow. Thanks, Das"*

**Example 2.** Draft: *"Hi Team, To track errors like 'Bad setup no mapping' or user unable to find
some batches we can Index certain values which can more confidently tell the state of the batch... So
the soltuio to this is: 1. Identify the the 'key identifier" IA value 2. Add it to index(So that its
searable) 3. Next time error happens search the entire IA server for this value."*
Sent: *"Hi Team, To track errors like 'Bad setup no mapping' or cases where users can't find certain
batches, we can index some values that can tell us the state of the batch more easily... Proposed
solution: 1. Identify the key IA value. 2. Add it to an index so it becomes searchable. 3. If a
similar issue happens in the future, search the IA server using that value. Thanks, Das"*

Both show: contractions, active voice with human subjects, direct labels ("Proposed solution:") over
hedged framing, and plain verbs replacing formal ones.

## For articles specifically

The site's teaching brief (motivation before mechanism, build intuition, one idea at a time) sits on
top of this voice, it does not replace it. When "more polished" is asked for, move one notch toward
formal, never toward corporate or Western-idiomatic. The comedy in the sketches is the one place
levity is deliberate; the prose stays plain, precise, and dense.

**Captions and front-matter descriptions must be short, simple, and concise.** No "hi-fi" English.
The sketch itself carries the humour; the caption just states the point plainly in a line or two.
Descriptions are one short sentence, not a paragraph. Example rewrite: *"You can bolt a jet engine to
one computer. It will not end well. The bored little machines next to it were the answer all along."*
became *"You can't upgrade one machine forever. Use many cheap ones instead."*
